# The 104-Week Plan

**From "finished the course" to a key player on the UAE AI frontier.**

Start: Monday 28 September 2026 · End: Sunday 24 September 2028
Total committed time: **~1,190 hours** over 24 months.

This document is the personal counterpart to [`ROADMAP.md`](ROADMAP.md). That one is about
closing gaps in the *course*. This one is about closing the gap between the reader and the
frontier — with dates, hours per day, and metrics that can fail.

---

## 0. The honest version of the goal

The stated goal is "as competent as anyone else in the field of AI at the frontiers." Taken
literally, that is not reachable and chasing it literally is this plan's biggest failure risk.

The arithmetic: a researcher at a frontier lab with a PhD and five years in the job has roughly
**15,000 focused hours** in the field. This plan buys **1,190**. Eight percent of theirs. No
sequencing of those 1,190 hours makes you their peer across the whole field, and any plan that
implies otherwise is selling you something.

But "the field of AI" is not one field, and the frontier is not one place. There is a narrow
intersection where 1,190 well-aimed hours *does* make you world-class, because almost nobody is
standing there:

> **AI systems that make or support high-stakes decisions about people — hiring, promotion,
> capability, workforce planning — built to be defensible under a risk-based regulatory regime,
> working in both Arabic and English.**

Count the people worldwide who are genuinely deep in *all four* of those: frontier ML practice,
human-capital domain expertise, AI regulation and assurance, and bilingual Arabic/English
deployment. It is dozens, not thousands. You already own one of the four outright, which is the
part that takes other people a decade. The UAE's national strategy needs domain-deep builders in
that intersection considerably more than it needs one more AI generalist.

So the target this plan actually optimises for:

**Globally credible in AI for high-stakes human-capital decisions. Nationally consequential in the
UAE. Technically honest about everything outside that.** That last clause is not a consolation
prize — it is the credential. The people who command respect at the frontier are the ones who are
genuinely deep somewhere and precise about their edges. The ones who claim the whole field get
found out in one conversation.

That is the goal below. The three milestones you set are kept exactly as you wrote them.

### Why this intersection, specifically

- **Regulation is creating the demand.** The UAE moved from the 2024 AI Charter (non-binding
  principles) toward a risk-tiered AI framework through 2026. Reporting on the framework and its
  four-tier classification is not fully consistent yet, so treat the specifics as *to be verified
  at source* rather than settled — but the direction is unambiguous, and it matches the EU AI Act's
  treatment of employment AI as high-risk. An automated system that ranks, filters, or rejects job
  applicants is close to the definition of a high-risk system in every framework now being written.
  Somebody has to be the person who can both build those systems and defend them. Right now, in
  this region, that person does not exist in any number.
- **Domain expertise is a research asset here, not a hobby.** In most AI niches your HR background
  is colour. In *measuring whether an AI system judges people fairly and accurately*, knowing what
  a competency framework is, how assessors actually disagree, and what a defensible hiring decision
  looks like is the scarce input. The ML is the commodity part.
- **It does not require a GPU cluster.** Original, publishable work on evaluation, measurement, and
  assurance is possible on a laptop and a few hundred dollars of cloud. Original work on frontier
  pretraining is not. Pick the frontier you can actually reach from here.
- **It is an export.** A bilingual benchmark and assurance method for employment AI is useful to
  every GCC state and to any multinational operating here. National-strategy relevance follows from
  that, not from proximity to it.

## 1. The time budget

You asked for hours per day. Here is the shape, and then the reasoning.

### The standing week (Phase 2 and 3 — 12 hours)

| Day | Hours | Block type |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | 1.5 | Learn — one new concept or technique, nothing else |
| Tuesday | 1.5 | Learn |
| Wednesday | 1.5 | Learn |
| Thursday | 1.5 | Learn |
| Friday | 2.0 | **The hard thing** — the part you have been avoiding |
| Saturday | 3.0 | Build — uninterrupted, ends with a commit |
| Sunday | 1.0 | Ship & plan — write the week up publicly, update metrics, plan the next six blocks |
| **Total** | **12.0** | |

Phase 1 runs at **10 hours** (drop Friday, Saturday to 2.5) to build the habit before loading it.
Phase 3 keeps 12 and adds **four sprint weeks per year at ~25 hours**, taken as annual leave, for
the things that do not fit in three-hour slices — a training run you have to babysit, a paper
deadline, a conference.

### Why this shape and not another

**Why 1.5 hours on weekdays, not one or three.** Below about 75 minutes you spend the block
reloading context and never reach the work. Above two hours, on a weekday, before or after a full
job, you are not learning — you are sitting. 1.5 hours is roughly 20 minutes of reload and 70
minutes of actual thinking, and it is small enough that you will still do it on a bad day. The
plan only works if it survives bad days; 104 weeks contains a great many of them.

**Why Friday is separate.** This assumes the UAE half-day Friday. It is the only weekday block
long enough for something genuinely uncomfortable, and every skill jump in this plan sits behind
something uncomfortable — reading the paper properly instead of the summary, debugging the training
run, writing the thing with your name on it. Left to a normal block, that work gets deferred
forever, because there is always something easier to learn. Giving it a named slot is the whole
trick. If your Friday is a full working day, move this block to Saturday morning and make Saturday
5 hours.

**Why Sunday is only one hour, and why it is not optional.** This is the compounding hour. Writing
up a week forces you to find out whether you actually understood it — the gap between "I followed
that" and "I can explain that" is where most self-taught learning quietly fails. It is also the
entire position-building track for the first year, at one hour a week. Skip it and you arrive at
Phase 3 technically capable and completely invisible, which costs a year to fix.

**Why 12 and not 20.** Twenty hours a week alongside a career and a life has roughly a three-month
half-life. Twelve is survivable for two years, and two years at twelve beats four months at twenty
by a factor of about six. The binding constraint on this plan is not your capacity to learn. It is
104 consecutive weeks of consistency. Every design choice above is subordinated to that.

### Totals

| Phase | Weeks | Dates | Hours/week | Leave | Phase hours |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 · Translator | 1–13 | 28 Sep – 27 Dec 2026 | 10 | — | ~130 |
| 2 · Builder | 14–52 | 28 Dec 2026 – 26 Sep 2027 | 12 | 3 weeks | ~432 |
| 3 · Frontier | 53–104 | 27 Sep 2027 – 24 Sep 2028 | 12 + 4 sprints | 4 weeks | ~628 |
| **Total** | **104** | **2 years** | | | **~1,190** |

For scale: 1,190 hours is about **30 full-time weeks**, spread thin enough to be compatible with a
career. That is the trade. It is slower in calendar time and it is the only version that finishes.

## MILESTONE 1 · The Translator
### Weeks 1–13 · 28 Sep – 27 Dec 2026 · 10 h/week · ~130 hours

> *Confident with AI tools in a general sense, able to explain most common AI concepts, and able
> to educate leadership on the potential uses of AI and implementation.*

The goal of this phase is a specific and underrated thing: to become the person in the building
whose explanation of AI is *load-bearing* — leadership makes decisions based on it, and those
decisions turn out to be right. That requires mechanism, not analogy. Analogy gets you through one
meeting; mechanism lets you answer the follow-up question, and the follow-up question is where
credibility is actually won or lost.

### Minor milestones

| Weeks | Focus | Ship by end of the block |
|---|---|---|
| 1–3 | Ch 1–3 · labs 01–03 · tool fluency in parallel (projects, structured prompting, artifacts, your real work moved onto AI) | A written personal tool stack + your first measured time saving |
| 4–6 | Ch 4–9 (model zoo) · labs 04–05 | **"How AI actually works"** — a 6-slide leadership explainer that contains zero hype and one diagram you can defend |
| 7–9 | Ch 10–12 · lab 06 (train a GPT on your own machine) | **HR AI Opportunity Map** — 20 real processes in your function, each scored on: is this a language problem? · error tolerance · reversibility of a mistake · does the data exist? |
| 10–11 | Ch 13–15 · labs 07–08 · read the three papers | **Draft AI use policy** for the HR function, organised by risk tier and human-oversight requirement |
| 12–13 | Consolidate · deliver · defend | A live 45-minute leadership session, and two use cases you killed in writing |

The Opportunity Map in weeks 7–9 is the phase's real deliverable. Those four scoring questions are
not generic — they are exactly the four that separate an AI project that works from one that
becomes an expensive apology, and by week 9 you will understand *why* each one matters at the
mechanism level. Most organisations score projects on excitement. You will have scored yours on
error tolerance and reversibility, which is the same list a regulator will use.

### Success metrics

1. **The whiteboard test.** From memory, in under 10 minutes, draw pretraining → SFT → RLHF →
   inference and say what each stage changes about the model. A technical person confirms it is
   correct, not just plausible.
2. **The no-notes test.** Field ten unscripted leadership questions — *will it replace recruiters?
   is our data safe? why did it make that up? what does it cost per use? what happens when it's
   wrong about a candidate?* — with a mechanism-level answer to each.
3. **The delivery test.** One 45-minute leadership session delivered, and **at least one decision
   demonstrably changed** as a result. Applause is not the metric; a changed decision is.
4. **The kill test.** Two proposed AI use cases you argued *against*, in writing, with a technical
   reason. Enthusiasm is abundant and worthless. Calibrated refusal is the scarce thing, and it is
   what gets you invited to the next conversation.
5. **The personal test.** You save a measured ≥4 hours a week of your own work through AI
   workflows. You cannot credibly advise on adoption you have not done.
6. **The reading test.** Three papers read properly, not summarised: *Attention Is All You Need*,
   *InstructGPT*, and one on bias in automated résumé screening.

### The anti-metric

**If every explanation you give is an analogy, you have not reached this milestone.** "It's like
autocomplete" is where the field's public understanding already sits. If you cannot get one level
below the analogy on demand, you are a fluent consumer, not a translator — and the difference
becomes visible the first time somebody senior pushes back.

## MILESTONE 2 · The Builder
### Weeks 14–52 · 28 Dec 2026 – 26 Sep 2027 · 12 h/week · ~432 hours

> *Able to articulate AI systems and build complex workflows surrounding the systems. Shows signs
> of competence with building AI systems.*

Nine months, four projects, escalating deliberately in difficulty *and* in consequence. The
sequence is chosen so each project forces a skill the next one depends on.

### The curriculum underneath

Python as a working tool rather than a reading language · git as a habit · the Claude API, tool
use, structured output · embeddings, chunking, hybrid search, reranking · **evaluation** ·
LoRA fine-tuning · data pipelines, versioning, PII handling · one cloud, one container, one
deployed service · cost and latency budgets · tracing and monitoring.

One of those is in bold because it is the entire separation between the two populations of people
currently claiming to build AI systems. **Evaluation is the boundary.** The people who can state
their system's accuracy, its failure modes, and its cost per call are building software. The people
who cannot are demoing. From the outside on a good day these look identical, and under any scrutiny
at all they do not.

### The four projects

**P2.1 · The Policy Answerer** — weeks 14–22
Retrieval over your real HR corpus: handbook, labour law, internal SOPs. Arabic and English.
Ships with a 100-question evaluation set with gold answers, mandatory citations, an explicit
refusal path for out-of-scope questions, and a measured accuracy number.
*Why first:* retrieval is the highest-value, lowest-risk use case in HR — nobody is harmed by a
wrong answer that cites its source and can be checked. It is the safe place to learn evaluation,
which is the skill everything after this depends on.

**P2.2 · The Structured Extraction Pipeline** — weeks 23–31
Interview transcripts and CVs to structured competency scorecards against a defined framework.
Ships with an explicit schema, agreement measured against human assessors, a **bias audit** across
name/gender/nationality perturbations of otherwise identical inputs, and a documented
human-in-the-loop design.
*Why second:* this is the high-risk category. Build it now, deliberately, with full defensibility,
while the stakes are internal — because this class of system is what Phase 3 is about and what the
regulation is aimed at. The bias audit here becomes the seed of your Phase 3 benchmark.

**P2.3 · The Agentic Workflow** — weeks 32–42
Multi-step, multi-tool: a workforce-planning assistant that queries an HRIS-shaped database, runs
headcount scenarios, and drafts a plan. Ships with tool schemas, failure handling, a trace layer, a
per-run cost budget, and a written specification of **what it must never do**.
*Why third:* agents fail in ways single calls do not — compounding errors, silent tool failures,
unbounded cost. You cannot learn that from a system with one step.

**P2.4 · First fine-tune, first deployment** — weeks 43–52
LoRA fine-tune an open model (Qwen, Llama, or Falcon — Falcon is Abu Dhabi's, and the adjacency is
worth something later) on a task the base model is measurably bad at. Prove on a held-out set that
it beat your best prompt. Then deploy one of P2.1–2.3 to real users who are not you.
*Why last:* fine-tuning is the most over-reached-for tool in the field. Doing it after three
projects of prompting and retrieval means you will understand why it is usually the wrong answer —
and be able to say so, with a number, when someone asks for it.

### Minor milestones

| Week | Gate |
|---|---|
| 18 | Your first eval set catches a regression you introduced on purpose |
| 22 | P2.1: ≥85% correct with citations on the 100-question set; ≥95% correct refusal out of scope |
| 26 | Bias audit running; you can state score variance across perturbed identical CVs and explain the residual |
| 31 | P2.2 agreement with human assessors ≥ agreement between two human assessors |
| 36 | Agent completes a 6-step task ≥90% of 50 runs, inside a fixed cost per run |
| 42 | Full tracing; you can answer "why did it do that" for any historical run, from logs alone |
| 48 | Fine-tune beats the prompt-engineered baseline on held-out data, written up |
| 52 | One system in real use by ≥10 people who are not you, with usage data |

Week 31's bar is the one to notice. "Is the AI good enough?" is unanswerable. "Does it agree with
assessor A at least as much as assessor B does?" is answerable, and it is the right question,
because the human baseline is the thing you are actually replacing. Most AI-in-HR vendors cannot
tell you this number. Knowing to ask it is a Phase 3 credential arriving early.

### Success metrics

1. **Ship test.** Three systems in real use; at least one making regulated-adjacent decisions with
   a documented risk assessment.
2. **Numbers test.** For every system you can state accuracy, failure modes, and cost per call
   without looking anything up.
3. **Review test.** A working ML engineer reviews one of your systems and their findings are about
   trade-offs, not fundamentals. (Ask for this explicitly around week 45. Pay for it if you must.)
4. **Architecture test.** You can whiteboard your own system including the two alternatives you
   rejected and why.
5. **Public test.** Two written pieces — one technical, one for an HR audience — and one talk at a
   UAE meetup or industry event.
6. **Money test.** One system with an ROI figure an executive has signed their name to.

### The anti-metric

**If none of your systems has an eval number, you are a power user with good taste, not a
builder.** This is the most common way people spend a year on this and arrive nowhere: a portfolio
of impressive demos, no measurements, no idea which ones actually work. The eval numbers are not
paperwork. They are the evidence that the thing you built does what you say it does, and without
them Phase 3 is not available to you at any number of hours.

## MILESTONE 3 · The Frontier Contributor
### Weeks 53–104 · 27 Sep 2027 – 24 Sep 2028 · 12 h/week + 4 sprints · ~628 hours

> *Ability to competitively train and develop AI systems with complex real-world applications that
> deliver high value to organisations, leading to becoming an important key player in the UAE AI
> frontier and strategy.*

Two tracks, in parallel, both mandatory. Depth without position makes you invisible — excellent
work nobody encounters. Position without depth makes you one of the several hundred people in this
region already describing themselves as an AI leader, which is a crowded and short-lived role.
Together they are the thing you asked for.

### Depth track — ~60% of hours (~375 h)

Real training, not only adaptation: a full fine-tune · continued pretraining on a domain corpus ·
preference tuning (DPO) on preferences **you collected** · distillation of a large model into a
small cheap one · and one reproduction from scratch (nanoGPT to GPT-2-small quality, or a paper's
headline result).

Then one frontier sub-area, deep enough to contribute to. **Recommendation: evaluation and
assurance for AI systems that make high-stakes decisions about people.**

Why that one, over the alternatives:
- It is a genuinely open problem. Nobody has solved measuring whether these systems are fair and
  accurate in deployment, and it is not solved by any amount of compute.
- Regulatory demand is arriving on a known schedule, in this jurisdiction, right now.
- Your domain expertise is the scarce input rather than the decoration.
- It is reachable on a laptop plus modest cloud spend. Original work in pretraining is not.
- It is the natural output of Phases 1 and 2 — the Opportunity Map's scoring criteria and P2.2's
  bias audit are already the first two pieces of it.

Credible alternatives if that one goes cold: Arabic and multilingual model capability (TII/Falcon
adjacency); long-horizon agent reliability; interpretability of decision-making models. Choose by
week 56 and then stop choosing — reopening this question at week 80 costs a year.

### Position track — ~40% of hours (~250 h)

- **Publish.** Six or more substantive technical write-ups. You will have twelve months of Sunday
  hours behind you by now; this is the compounding paying out.
- **Ship the benchmark.** One open-source release, and it should be a **public bilingual benchmark
  for AI systems making employment-relevant decisions, with a fairness and robustness suite.**
  This is the single highest-leverage artifact available to you in the entire plan. Nobody owns it.
  A benchmark is how an outsider becomes a fixed reference point instead of a participant — the
  people who make the measuring stick get consulted by everyone who has to be measured, including
  regulators, and it cannot be taken away from you by someone with a better CV.
- **Connect.** MBZUAI, TII, G42/Core42, the AI Office, GITEX, the HR-tech and legal-tech
  communities. Target three substantive collaborations, meaning shared work, not shared coffee.
- **Speak.** Four talks, escalating: community meetup → industry conference → national event →
  policy or government forum.
- **Advise.** Contribute to at least one consultation, standard, or policy input on AI in
  employment. This is where the word "strategy" in your goal is actually cashed.

### Minor milestones

| Week | Gate |
|---|---|
| 56 | Specialty area chosen and committed, in writing, with the reasoning |
| 60 | A continued-pretraining or full fine-tune run completed on real domain data — with a loss curve you can read and a held-out eval |
| 68 | A distilled model within 5% of the large model's eval score at under 10% of the cost |
| 76 | Benchmark v0.1 public, with at least three models evaluated on it |
| 84 | First external citation, fork, or invited use of your work |
| 90 | A talk at a national-level event or a government/policy forum |
| 96 | A system in production with a measured organisational value figure — cost, time, quality, or compliance risk retired |
| 104 | You are consulted on an AI decision you did not initiate |

Week 68 is the one organisations care most about and nobody talks about. "Same quality, one tenth
the cost" is how AI goes from a pilot to a line item that survives a budget review. It is also a
genuine training skill — you cannot prompt your way to it.

Week 104 is the real test of the whole plan. Every other metric you can drive by pushing.
Being consulted on something you did not start is the one that has to come to you, and it is the
only honest definition of "key player" available.

### Success metrics

1. **Training test.** You have trained models on real data, not only called them, and can explain
   every hyperparameter choice and what the loss curve told you.
2. **Beat-the-baseline test.** In your specialty, one documented, reproducible result that beats
   the obvious approach.
3. **Reference test.** Someone you have never met cites, uses, or forks your work.
4. **Inbound test.** Unsolicited invitations to speak, advise, or collaborate. You have stopped
   being the one asking.
5. **Value test.** At least one system with a signed-off organisational value figure — ideally
   seven figures AED, or a compliance exposure formally retired.
6. **Peer test.** You can hold a technical conversation with an MBZUAI or TII researcher in which
   they treat you as a peer *on your specialty*, while both of you know you are not a peer on
   theirs. **This is what the goal looks like when it is real.** Depth where you claim it,
   precision about the rest. Anyone who claims more is one question away from being found out.

### The anti-metric

**If your position is talks and no artifacts, you have become an AI thought leader** — a role this
market is already oversupplied with and which has a visibly shortening shelf life. The test is
simple and worth applying every quarter: what exists, with your name on it, that someone else
could run, fork, or measure something against? If the honest answer is "slides," the depth track
has been quietly abandoned and needs to be the only thing you do for a quarter.

## 3. Operating rules

These matter more than the content. The content is available to anyone; the consistency is not.

**Daily.** One thing per block. First ten minutes re-reading yesterday's notes — never fight for
context twice. No block without a note at the end of it, even one line.

**Friday.** The hard thing. If Friday's block felt comfortable, you chose the wrong task.

**Saturday.** Ends with a commit. Not a finished feature — a commit. Something that existed only in
your head at 9am must exist outside it by noon.

**Sunday.** Write up the week publicly. Three hundred words is enough. Update the metrics table.
Plan the next six blocks by name, so Monday morning requires no decisions.

**Every four weeks.** Review against the current phase's minor milestones. Behind by more than two
weeks twice consecutively → **cut scope, do not add hours.** Adding hours is how this ends in month
five. Cutting scope is how it finishes.

**Every phase** ends with something that exists outside your head and can be examined by someone
else. No exceptions, no "I understand it but haven't written it up." Unwritten understanding
reliably turns out to be less than it felt like.

## 4. Re-plan triggers

Written in advance, because the moment they fire is the moment judgment is worst.

| Trigger | Response |
|---|---|
| Phase 1 takes more than 16 weeks | The hours are wrong, not you. Drop to 8 h/week and extend the calendar. Do not quit and do not compress. |
| No eval number for P2.1 by week 22 | Stop building entirely. Three weeks on evaluation alone. Everything downstream depends on this and nothing downstream works without it. |
| Two consecutive four-week reviews behind | Cut one project from the phase. Four projects done properly beats six done partially, every time. |
| No external artifact by week 60 | The position track has failed. It leads for a full quarter — publish, release, speak — before any new depth work. |
| Specialty area still unchosen at week 60 | Choose the recommended one and move. Deliberation past this point is avoidance wearing a lab coat. |
| Organisational access to real HR problems and data does not materialise | Substitute public and synthetic datasets. Cost: roughly three months of credibility, because "I built it on real data with real users" is doing a lot of work in Phase 2. Surface this in Phase 1, not Phase 2. |

## 5. What this plan assumes

Stated openly so they can be corrected rather than discovered.

1. **~12 hours a week is available and sustainable**, including through busy quarters. If the true
   figure is 8, this is a 3-year plan and that is fine — say so now and it gets re-dated.
2. **The UAE half-day Friday applies to you.** If not, the Friday block moves to Saturday morning.
3. **You can get access to real HR problems, real users, and — with appropriate governance — real
   data.** This is the single most important assumption in the document. It is what makes Phase 2
   worth four times a course.
4. **A few hundred dollars a year of cloud compute is available.** Phases 2 and 3 need GPU hours.
   This is the cheapest input in the plan and the one people strangely economise on.
5. **Publishing publicly under your own name is acceptable** to you and your employer. If not, the
   position track needs redesigning early — internal-only credibility does not reach a national
   ecosystem.
6. **The regulatory direction holds.** If the UAE's risk-tiered framework stalls, the demand-side
   argument weakens, though the fairness-and-measurement problem does not go away. Verify the
   current state of the framework at source during Phase 1 — do not take this document's word for
   it, or anyone's summary.

## 6. The one-paragraph version

Two years. Twelve hours a week. About 1,190 hours. Three months to become the person whose
explanation of AI leadership actually relies on; nine months to become someone who ships AI systems
with measured accuracy, cost, and failure modes; twelve months to train models and own the
measuring stick for AI that makes decisions about people in this region. You will not be a peer to
a frontier researcher across the field, and this plan does not pretend otherwise. You will be one
of a very small number of people in the world who is genuinely deep at the intersection of frontier
practice, human capital, and AI assurance — which is a place the UAE's strategy needs filled, and
which nobody is currently standing in.
